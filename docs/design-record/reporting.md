## From CLAUDE.md section: Schedule III captions — one vocabulary, and the screen is served it

- **The fixed-assets note is a MOVEMENT, and there is one of it.**
  `domain/reporting/fixed_asset_movement.py` computes opening gross block,
  additions, deductions, closing, the same four for accumulated depreciation,
  and net block at both ends — per asset class, from already-fetched rows, with
  no database handle. Three callers read it and none re-derives it: the
  year-end note (`routers/year_end_notes.py`), `GET
  /api/fixed-assets/movement`, and both year-end PDFs. Its rules are worth
  knowing before touching any of them. **Every asset, disposed included** — an
  asset sold during the year is a DEDUCTION, and filtering it out is what made
  last year's closing fail to tie to this year's opening; only a soft-deleted
  row is excluded, because migration 351 makes that a row created by mistake.
  **The CHARGE comes off the ledger** (`account_period_balances`, twelve
  pre-aggregated rows) and the per-class split from the register's own
  `accumulated_depreciation_paise − depreciation_fy_start_accum_paise`, which
  speaks only for the asset's CURRENT depreciation FY — so an earlier year
  reports `split_known = False` rather than a split that silently omits an
  asset, and **where the two disagree the difference is STATED**
  (`movement_gaps`), never absorbed. **With no financial year it reports the
  register AS IT STANDS**, closing figures only, and says so: a movement with
  no period is not a conservative answer, it is a wrong one. The caveats are
  rendered wherever the figures are — the Reports tab, the notes screen and
  both PDFs — because a movement shown without the sentence saying the ledger
  and the register disagree is exactly the disclosure a reader would rely on.

## From CLAUDE.md section: Reporting performance — the rule, not a preference

**PAGING IS NOT THE SAME AS BOUNDING, AND A RECONCILIATION NEEDED BOTH**
(BANK-07). `bank_reconciliation_service._account_txns` was correctly PAGED and
still read every transaction the account had ever carried, because `_classify`
did the period filtering in Python — so a client three years into an engagement
shipped three years of statement lines to answer a question about one month.
Paging stops a silent truncation; it does nothing about a read that is
proportional to the ledger. `_session_txns` is the bounded one and the four
`_classify` callers use it. **`_index_account_txns` was the worse of the two**
and had no finding: it resolved the handful of ids a CA had just ticked by
reading the whole account, where the answer is `len(txn_ids)` rows.
⚠️ **THE FINDING'S OWN SUGGESTED FIX WAS WRONG, and the reason generalises.**
"Apply the period predicate in the query" is the natural reading and it breaks
the `reconciled` bucket — the ONE bucket `_classify` deliberately does not
date-filter, because a cheque written on 28 March and cleared on 3 April is
claimed by the April session whatever its own date says. A plain `BETWEEN`
would take its amount out of a tie-out that has already been certified. So the
fetch is the UNION of what the four buckets need: **the period, OR claimed by
this session**. Before narrowing any read, check which consumer does NOT apply
the filter you are about to push down.
**TWO QUERIES RATHER THAN ONE `or_`**, which is the opposite trade from the one
`_account_txns` records in its own docstring — there the rejected second
crossing was the SAME SIZE as the first, here it is bounded by what one session
has claimed and it removes an unbounded scan. It also keeps a PostgREST
or-expression out of the code, which matters because two separate fakes stand in
for the database in this suite and each would need to parse one.
**`_classify` IS UNCHANGED and still filters in Python**: it is the definition
of the four buckets, and a narrowed fetch must not become a second, quieter copy
of it. A test asserts the narrowed and unbounded fetches classify IDENTICALLY on
a fixture with a row in each limb.
**AND THE GUARD THAT BROKE WAS NAMING A METHOD AGAIN.**
`test_one_fetch_serves_all_four_buckets` counted calls to `_account_txns` and
expected exactly one — a spelling of "no bucket gets its own query" — so it
failed on a change that made the thing it cares about strictly better. It counts
reads of the TABLE now, bounded by a number that does not grow with the buckets.
That is the fourth time this pattern has been fixed; write the rule, not a
spelling of it.

**A read that IS a row set has its own rule, and it is one line: page it.**
PostgREST caps a response at ~1000 rows (`db-max-rows`) and reports nothing
when it does, so a truncated read is indistinguishable from a complete one and
every figure computed from it is confidently wrong. `core/db_paging.fetch_all`
is the one helper — keyset, never OFFSET, stopping on a short page — and it is
the one to import; no module carries a private `_paginate_all` copy now (ten hand-rolled pagers remain in the frozen list in `tests/test_no_module_carries_its_own_pager.py`),
and adding another is the thing not to do. Two guards state the rule rather
than a spelling of it: `tests/test_paginated_selects_carry_their_key.py` fails a
paged query whose `.select()` omits the cursor column (which works perfectly
until the thousandth row and then cannot advance), and it scans `fetch_all`
alongside the private copies — it did not, so for a while a call site MOVED OUT
of the rule by moving to the shared helper. Two traps at the call site:
`fetch_all` imposes its own `ORDER BY id`, so an ordering the endpoint wants is
applied to the rows it got BACK, never inside the paged query; and sort keys are
coalesced, because a nullable column such as `fixed_assets.asset_code` raises
`TypeError` in Python where the database sorted it happily.
**AND ITS FIRST ARGUMENT IS A CALLABLE, WHICH TWO CALL SITES FORGOT.**
`fetch_all(make_query, key="id", *, label, stats)` CALLS `make_query` once per
page — it has to, because a builder is stateful and reusing one stacks each
page's `.gt(key, cursor)` on the last — so the projection goes INSIDE a
`def one_page(): return db.table(...).select(...)`. Handing it the builder
raises `TypeError: '_Query' object is not callable` on page one, and a third
positional argument raises before the body runs at all. Both happened, and
**neither was visible from its own tests, for two different reasons** — which
is what makes it a class rather than a pair.
`services/reorder_service._catalogue` passed the builder, so the reorder report
has never run against a
database: `routers/inventory.reorder_report` catches it and answers "Unable to
load the reorder report. Please try again.", which reads as transient, and the
mock suite could not reach it because that router's `_USE_MOCK` branch passes
`db=None` and `assess` short-circuits to an empty answer BEFORE the fetch.
`services/hub_service._sum_paise` passed three positional arguments, and its
three tiles are computed inside `_safely`, which swallows a tile's exception by
design — so Sales, Purchases and TDS rendered "—" on the hub. One hidden by a
router's broad `except`, one by a deliberate per-tile one.
`tests/test_fetch_all_is_given_something_it_can_call.py` states the rule on the
ARGUMENTS at every call site, so a module nobody thought of is covered the day
it is written. **The durable half is the second reason: a service whose job is
to FETCH needs a test that FETCHES.** A source scan cannot see an arity error,
and a `db is None` mock branch is not the code that runs in production —
`tests/test_the_reorder_report_runs_against_a_database.py` and
`tests/test_the_hub_actually_answers.py` are the two that now do.
⚠️ **The same test found a THIRD null with no exception behind it**, which is
the `table_4a_gaps` discipline on a screen: the hub's payload defines
`answerable: true` with a null signal as *the fetch for this tile failed*, and
`Tile.no_firm_signal_because` had promised since it was written that the CLIENT
hub answers Inventory — while `_signals` computed nothing for it at any scope.
So a tile nobody had ASKED for was indistinguishable from one that had been
asked and failed. A nil meaning "nothing to do", a nil meaning "nobody can
tell" and a nil meaning "nobody looked" are three different things, and a
payload with a three-state contract has to be exercised to find out which one
it is emitting.

**THE RULE IS ABOUT THE BROWSER TOO, and that is where it was still being
broken.** Everything above is written for `apps/api`, and the frontend reaches
~83 tables directly over PostgREST — where the same ~1000-row cap applies, with
the same silence. `/accounting/budget` read `journal_lines` joined to
`journal_entries`, FIRM-WIDE, once per quarter, unpaged, and computed the
actuals in the browser: on any client with real volume every figure was short
by an unknown amount and every variance wrong, confidently, with no error
(ACC-06). The answer is one row per Revenue and Expense account — about fifty —
so it is now `GET /api/accounting/budgets`, reading `account_period_balances`
through **`ReportingService.period_net_by_account`**, which is the primitive to
reach for whenever one screen needs SEVERAL windows over the same accounts: it
fetches the chart and the buckets ONCE and projects each window through the
same `_passbook_lines` the Trial Balance uses. Four `trial_balance` calls would
have been eight Singapore-to-Mumbai round trips for one screen.
`core.ist_clock.fy_quarters` keeps the windows month-aligned (derived from
`fy_bounds`, never restating April), which is what lets the pre-aggregated
buckets answer exactly with no edge month to replay.

**AN OFFSET-PAGED READ NEEDS A UNIQUE TOTAL ORDERING, and the ordering a screen
already had is usually not one.** Postgres guarantees nothing without an ORDER
BY, and a NON-unique one — `invoice_date`, `account_name`, `client_name` — lets
ties land either side of a page boundary, so a row can come back twice or never.
The fix is a tiebreaker LAST, `.order("id")`, which need not be in the
projection and so changes no exported column. `app/risks` (six reads),
`app/accounting/receivables` and `app/accounting/coa-export` build a CSV
straight from these reads and had none of this: `compliance_calendar` carries a
row per obligation per client per period, so a 50-client book passes 1000 inside
one year, and the file opened, looked complete, and was short by whatever the
cap removed. `app/accounting/recurring` is paged too and is NOT one of them —
its Export reads `templates` from the API and its PostgREST read feeds the
account dropdowns — which the guard says rather than keeping one list by
softening the claim. **The other 68 of the 102 files touching PostgREST still
carry a read that is neither paged nor bounded**, and are left as a finding
rather than swept: most are bounded in practice by one client or one month, a
screen that truncates is at least a screen somebody is looking at, and a budget
over 68 files is the shape that gets raised until it means nothing.

- **THE DAY BOOK IS A PAGE THE DATABASE CUTS, THE SCREEN AND THE FILE READ THE SAME FUNCTION, AND A DRAFT IS NOT A POSTING** (POST-A-091, migration 517, 18-10-2026). The Day Book screen read `journal_entries` over PostgREST in the browser with every entry's lines embedded, for the whole period, and summed a debit column out of them: a read proportional to the ledger to answer a list (the shape the Journal tab measured at 1.3 s a page on a 12,836-entry client), and with no server function there was nothing for a PDF or an Excel file to be the same as, so the first book an auditor asks for had no printable form (accounting-16 had named exactly that as the reason it was left out). `public.day_book_page(firm, client, start, end, newest_first, limit, offset)` returns one page as jsonb, each entry's debit and credit totals summed in the database so the lines never cross the wire, and over the WHOLE window and never the page the entry count, the totals and the documents the entries came from with a count each (the Source filter reads that, so its options are not just whatever the loaded page held). It is the shape of `account_ledger_page`: SECURITY INVOKER so row security covers both tables, read-only, no anon, with `builders.day_book` as the mock-mode twin and `tests/test_day_book_sql_parity_pg.py` running 14 scenarios through both in both orders (inclusive window ends, a draft and a deleted entry only the database holds, same-day entries ordered by recorded time against insertion order, a tie broken by id alone, an entry with no lines, a null reference, and source names a locale and a byte order sort differently: `Opening` against `manual`, `sales_credit_note` against `sales_invoice`, which is why every ordering string is `COLLATE "C"`) plus the pages reassembled into the unpaged book. **A DRAFT IS NOT A POSTING.** The screen listed an unposted manual draft with a Draft status; the function lists posted, live entries only, because a Day Book is "every posting in the period" and a draft has moved nothing in any account. The Status column is gone from the Day Book (it would say Posted on every row), and the Journal tab, which lists what a person wrote, still shows a draft and is where it is posted or discarded. The dashboard's "Posted Entries (FY)" card, which sends the CA here, counts exactly these. **WITH A DATABASE THERE IS NO FALLBACK AFTER A FAILURE**: the ledger falls back to its Python builder when its function fails, which costs one account's entries; a Day Book fallback would read the client's whole period and their lines silently, which is the read this removes, so `ReportingService.day_book` raises and the endpoint answers "could not be read" and never an empty book (an AST test holds that the builder sits only on the no-database branch and every handler re-raises). **THE FILE IS THE SCREEN.** `GET /api/accounting/day-book` (client required and scope-checked, strict dates, a limit the server clamps to 1,000, `has_more` from the server's own count, `max_limit` served so the browser carries no copy) feeds the table through the shared window reader and the list-window notice, newest first; `GET /api/report-exports/day-book` reads the very same function a page at a time in chronological order to the last entry, one amount per entry (the total of its debit lines, which equals its credit lines because the kernel refuses an unbalanced entry), and REFUSES a book whose entries do not add to the window's count and totals (a short read), whose debits differ from its credits, or that holds an entry out of balance (named by reference and date), and a period past 6,000 entries for a PDF or 50,000 for a sheet is refused in words after ONE page (the ledger's measured ceilings: a Day Book row is the cheap single-line case). The file is for the PERIOD on screen, never for the window, and the screen says where the rest can be had. The architecture note's list of exposed reports names it. Deliberately NOT done: the Journal tab (manual entries) still reads straight over PostgREST, a different list with its own keyset reader; the Source filter acts on the loaded window (the notice says when the window is cut short), not on a server-side source parameter; a firm-wide Day Book does not exist (one client's books, as the ledger's export). **AN INDEPENDENT READ OF THIS CHANGE FOUND SIX LOW POINTS AND FIVE WERE REAL.** (1) The window notice outlived a failed read and described the period before; a failure clears it. (2) "Show more" is an ask keyed by client and period, so changing either starts from the server's window in the same render (an effect that reset it afterwards read the new period twice and the slower answer won), and only the newest read writes the screen. (3) The table's own CSV is not offered on the Day Book, because it would be a partial file of the window with nothing to say so: the PDF and Excel files beside it are the period. (4) The ordering renders the recorded moment in UTC before comparing it as text, because `timestamptz::text` is written in the session's zone and in a zone with daylight saving the later instant of the repeated autumn hour writes a smaller text (proved under New York, and failing on the old expression); `account_ledger_page` (migrations 283 and 400) has the same weakness and was left for when it is next touched. (5) A query pattern of digits admits `2026-02-30`, which reached the database's date cast as a 400 worded "please try again", or a 500 on the export route; `core.validators.real_day_problem` matches the shape and asks the calendar (not `fromisoformat`, which also reads `20260230` and an ISO week date on newer Pythons) and both routes answer 422 in words. Not changed: the service does not repeat the route's client-scope check (`ledger` has the same shape and both callers check first).

- **A READ THAT FEEDS A FIGURE PAGES EVEN WHEN ITS SCREEN IS NOT AN EXPORT, AND THE RULE NAMES THE (SCREEN, TABLE)** (POST-A-135, first slice). The existing browser rule was about downloads: a screen that builds a CSV from PostgREST pages every read it makes. A recount on 18-10-2026 (every `.from(...).select(...)` in app, components and lib with no limit, range, single, count, id lookup or pager) found 82 unpaged reads in 54 files, and six of them feed a count, a total or a balance on a screen that also makes other reads, so they could not join a list of screens that page everything: the GST dashboard's `compliance_calendar` (every GSTR-1, 3B and 9 row of every client and every year, ordered OLDEST first, so the silent cut at ~1000 rows dropped the NEWEST filings, which the counts above the table are about: fifty monthly filers reach 1,250 rows a year), the income-tax dashboard's ITR rows, the attendance year behind the leaves-used total (one row per employee per month, so ninety employees pass 1,000 and the balance left came out too HIGH), the leave balances, and a client's `tds_deductions` on the TDS tab (read twice; a client paying a hundred vendors a month passes 1,000 in ten months, newest first, so every tab total was short by what fell past the cut). The month's attendance roster, the same table, is paged with them. All are `selectAll` reads with a unique `.order("id")` tiebreaker now (the embedded parent in the two calendar reads is a to-one join, so the offset pager is right and the keyset one is not needed). `PAGED_READS_THAT_FEED_A_FIGURE` in `scripts/an-export-reads-every-row.test.ts` names each (screen, table) and asks the SOURCE: every read of that table on that screen sits directly inside a pager call and carries the tiebreaker, with a floor on the number of reads examined and negative controls (a paged read, an unpaged one, one without a tiebreaker and a write are told apart). **It is deliberately a named list and not a budget over the 82**: most are bounded by one client, party, document or month, and a budget over that many files gets raised until it means nothing. The remainder is the ledger line, with the few worth a later look named (the firm-wide document lists, the vendor and customer pickers after a Tally import, the chart-of-accounts pickers).

- **A READ THAT IS THE ONLY WAY TO FIND OR CHECK A RECORD PAGES TOO, ON ITS OWN LIST, AND A DUPLICATE CHECK THAT IGNORED A FAILED READ WOULD HAVE INSERTED THE FILE AGAIN** (POST-A-135, second slice, 19-10-2026). A rescan found 70 unbounded reads (the first slice had moved the count, and a scan that did not recognise a pager written with a block body or a generic type argument had overcounted). Ten of them GROW WITH A FIRM'S VOLUME and are now paged with a unique `.order("id")` tiebreaker, in two kinds kept apart so neither claim is softened to cover both. **A figure:** the billing page's `fee_invoices` and `fee_receipts`, whose revenue, outstanding, overdue, issued and draft totals are added up in the browser, newest first. A firm's fee invoices pass 1,000 in under two years on a 50-client book, and the cut took the OLDEST, so revenue came out short and an old unpaid invoice was missing from "outstanding" altogether. They join `PAGED_READS_THAT_FEED_A_FIGURE`. **The only way to find or check a record,** on a second list, `PAGED_READS_THAT_ARE_THE_ONLY_WAY_TO_FIND_OR_CHECK_A_RECORD`, held by the same assertions: the chart-of-accounts import's existing-codes read (the duplicate check, so a code past the cut was inserted a second time; and the read's ERROR was ignored, so a failed read left no existing codes at all and the whole file went in over a chart that was already there, which now stops the import with a sentence), the firm-level Schedule III mapping and account-group screens (an account past the cut could not be mapped or regrouped), the ageing page's markable accounts (marking puts a balance into a statutory disclosure), and the four document lists (the firm's documents, a client's vault, its documents tab and the portal's shared list), newest first, so the cut took the oldest documents from the only list they can be opened from. **Read and left alone:** the purchase-bill and sales-invoice lookups by number (chunks of 200, so bounded), the vendor statements pick-list (already paged; a generic type argument hid its pager from a scan) and the reads scoped to one client, period or document. A third test checks the two lists never name the same (screen, table). Negative controls: five mutants (the pager removed from the billing invoices, the import's check and the portal's documents; the tiebreaker removed from the billing receipts and a client's documents) each fail the guard, and the baseline passes again once they are undone. About 60 unbounded reads remain, recorded in the ledger line. No migration; a book under 1,000 rows sees no change except that a failed read now stops the chart import. **Not driven in a browser.**
- **A ROW-SECURITY POLICY ASKS WHO IS CALLING ONCE PER QUERY AND NOT ONCE PER ROW, AND THE RULE IS A TEST OVER pg_policy** (migration 525, resolves the measurement half of POST-A-219, 20-10-2026). Found while measuring migration 524: a policy such as `firm_id = get_my_firm_id()` or `my_permission('payroll', 'read', 'Manager')` calls a STABLE function whose arguments are constants, written bare, and Postgres calls it for EVERY ROW a query touches; only a call inside a scalar sub-select, `(SELECT fn(...))`, is planned as an InitPlan and evaluated once. The helpers are SECURITY DEFINER functions with a `SET search_path`, so they cannot be inlined either. **Measured on the migrated schema, 100,000 rows, `SELECT count(*)` as a signed-in session** (what a browser read over PostgREST is, and the API when `USE_USER_JWT` is on): the firm policy 5,537 ms bare against 13 ms wrapped; a `my_permission` tier policy 61,115 ms bare against 6,262 ms wrapped, both with the firm policy still bare beside it (so about 5.5 s of the 6.3 s is the firm policy, and the tier policy's own wrapped cost is under a second); the assignment scope `can_access_client(client_id)` 13,232 ms as a Partner against 12 ms for the equivalent set-based form. 603 policy expressions called `get_my_firm_id()`, 339 `my_permission(...)`, 183 `my_role_at_least(...)`, 128 `get_my_role()` and 108 `my_internal_client_id()`, so every table the browser reads directly paid about 55 microseconds a row for the firm policy alone before a filter ran. Supabase's performance advisor names the pattern (`auth_rls_initplan`); migration 008 had applied it to a few `auth.uid()` policies. **525 rewrites the catalog, not the migrations that wrote the policies:** a `DO` block walks `pg_policy` in `public` and wraps every call of `get_my_firm_id`, `get_my_role`, `get_my_user_id`, `my_internal_client_id`, `my_permission`, `my_role_at_least`, `auth.uid`, `auth.jwt` and `auth.role` that has no argument or constants only, in the USING and the WITH CHECK, with `ALTER POLICY` (never drop-and-create, which opens a window with no policy), skips a call already preceded by `SELECT ` (so a second run changes nothing), and closes with a check that no bare call survives. `ALTER POLICY` takes an ACCESS EXCLUSIVE lock on its table and the migration is one transaction, so every table it touches is locked until it commits (about half a second for the 796 here, which in-flight requests wait for); the block sets a `lock_timeout` of 15 seconds for its own transaction so that a long-running transaction fails the migration (to be re-run) instead of queueing every later request behind it. 796 policies changed; names, kinds, commands and roles did not. **Why the catalog:** the history of `CREATE POLICY` statements is long, some replaced by later ones, some with a production-only twin, and a rewrite that restated them would have to guess which wording is live. **The proof it is the same predicate is not the argument that a STABLE function cannot change between rows, it is the role-by-table matrix** (101 tables x 10 actors x 4 operations = 4,040 cells, 0 deviating) and every other real-Postgres test that signs in, all unchanged, plus `tests/test_a_policy_asks_who_is_calling_once_per_query_and_not_once_per_row_pg.py`, which builds one synthetic policy of every SHAPE (AND, OR, NOT, CASE, a call inside an EXISTS, a WITH CHECK alone, an UPDATE with both, a JWT claim, a schema-qualified call; restrictive and permissive; a table and a policy whose names need quoting) **each on a table of its own** with only a permissive "everything" policy beside a restrictive one (an earlier draft put all eleven on one table, where restrictive policies AND together and four of the shapes could never change what anyone saw), records what seven actors (a Partner, a Manager and an Executive, a suspended member, a Partner of another firm, a signed-in person with no staff row, and the Manager's identity under a token whose role claim is not `authenticated`) can see, insert and update, FAILS a shape whose outcomes do not differ between actors, runs the migration, compares, runs it again, and runs the rollback and compares once more. **The rule is a test over `pg_policy`** (`test_no_policy_calls_a_helper_once_per_row`), so a policy a later migration writes bare fails and its author wraps it, and a plan test asserts a signed-in scan of five firm tables has an InitPlan and no helper inside a Filter. `scripts/db/guard_snapshot.py` hashes policy expressions after folding the wrapped form back to the bare one for every helper (it folded `auth.uid()` only), so the rewrite does not read as drift from the production snapshot (the committed 2026-09-03 snapshot was hashed with the old fold, so the two `firm_isolation` policies migration 046 wrote wrapped by hand, on `client_health_scores` and `client_health_overrides`, show as informational `policies_differ` lines until the snapshot is next captured); `tests/test_the_policy_wrapping_names_one_list_of_helpers.py` holds the migration's, the rollback's and the fold's lists equal. The rollback unwraps EVERY wrapped helper call in a `public` policy (the catalog cannot tell 525's wrapping from earlier hand-written wrapping, so the policies migrations 008, 045 to 047, 109, 153, 484, 506 and 510 wrote wrapped are unwrapped with them, answers unchanged, and a re-apply wraps them all again) and leaves migration 524's wrapped-by-design policies alone. **Deliberately NOT done:** `can_access_client((client_id)::text)`, called 394 times in 221 policies, takes the ROW's client and cannot be wrapped this way; it is the larger cost and the next migration (POST-A-297), and it is not a rewrite to make quietly: the function is SECURITY DEFINER so it can read `user_client_assignments` without recursing into that table's own policies, and a set-based form must keep that property. The policies of the `storage` schema belong to the Storage service and are not touched. Not run against production: the policies change there when the migration merges; a single `count(*)` on a large table as a signed-in session should be several times faster, and nothing should answer differently.
- **THE ASSIGNMENT SCOPE READS THE CALLER'S CLIENTS ONCE PER QUERY, AND THE DRIFT GUARD FOLDS THE REWRITE BACK TO THE CALL IT REPLACED** (migration 526, POST-A-297, 20-10-2026). The sequel to migration 525's measurements: `can_access_client((client_id)::text)`, which the 084 `<table>_assignment_scope` policies and later migrations' per-command and portal-composite policies call (221 policies on a migrated database, 394 calls with the USING and the WITH CHECK counted apart; the same figures as migration 525's entry), takes the ROW's client, so it cannot be wrapped in a sub-select the way the constant-argument helpers can, and it is a SECURITY DEFINER SQL function with a `SET search_path`, so Postgres cannot inline it. Each call runs `get_my_role()`, `get_my_user_id()` and, for anyone but a Partner, an `EXISTS` on `user_client_assignments`. **Measured, 100,000 rows, `SELECT count(*)` as a signed-in session with the firm policy already wrapped: a Partner 13,232 ms against 12 to 30 ms as a set, a Manager with one client 33,818 ms against 51 ms, the same 50,000 rows returned**, so about 130 and 340 microseconds a row, on every row a query reads before it filters. (Inlining the function was tried first: a plain SQL STABLE copy of its body, with no definer and no `SET`, is NOT inlined because the body contains a sub-select, and ran no faster.) 526 adds `my_assigned_client_ids()` (the caller's clients as a set of text, the shape of `my_employee_ids()`; SECURITY DEFINER as `can_access_client` is, so that it reads `user_client_assignments` whatever the caller's own grants and policies on that table are (084 left that table out of the assignment scope, as the source it reads); no argument, so outside migration 486's rule; nothing for a member who is no longer live; executable by anon on purpose, as the other RLS predicates are (migration 337): the policies it sits in have no TO clause, so a role without EXECUTE gets `permission denied for function` instead of no rows, and a first draft that revoked it did exactly that on 44 tables an anonymous key can select from, so a test now scans every table for it; for anon the function answers nothing) and rewrites, with `ALTER POLICY`, every call in `public` as `COALESCE(<c>::text IS NULL OR (SELECT get_my_role()) = 'Partner' OR <c>::text IN (SELECT my_assigned_client_ids()), false)`: the function's body with the row-independent parts evaluated once and the assignment lookup an uncorrelated `IN` planned as a hashed sub-plan, and the `COALESCE` keeping the answer a plain true or false as the function's was. The portal branches that sit beside it (`OR id IN (SELECT my_employee_ids())` and their like) are untouched. **The rewrite is the same predicate, shown by the role-by-table matrix (4,040 cells, 0 deviating) and by `tests/test_the_assignment_scope_reads_the_callers_clients_once_per_query_pg.py`, with a rule over `pg_policy` that none is left:** the test writes nine policy shapes with the OLD call, each on a table of its own (FOR ALL with the real 084 shape, SELECT alone, SELECT with a portal-style OR branch, INSERT alone, UPDATE with a USING that differs from its WITH CHECK, DELETE alone, an EXISTS on a qualified column, a column not called client_id, two calls in one expression) and compares what a Partner, a Manager assigned to client A, a Manager assigned to nothing, an Executive assigned to A, a SUSPENDED Partner and a Partner of another firm can see, insert, update, move to another client and delete over rows of client A, client B and no client, with the migration applied, after the rollback and after a re-apply, and that a second run changes nothing; a shape that every firm member answers alike for the operation it governs fails the test. **The drift guard does not see it:** a rewrite that is not a pure wrap cannot be hashed away by the first fold, so `scripts/db/guard_snapshot.py` carries a second fold that writes the deparsed set-based text back as `can_access_client((<c>)::text)` before hashing (the `COALESCE` wrapper is what keeps a composite policy's grouping, because the deparser flattens a nested `OR`), and the test computes the folded hash of every policy (more than a thousand) with the migration applied and with it rolled back and requires them equal; the comparison with the production snapshot itself is `test_guards_match_production_pg`. The rollback writes the call back, restores the raw text of every policy exactly (checked), and drops the helper. The function itself stays: SQL functions and the API call it and its answer has not changed. **Deliberately NOT done:** the `storage` schema's policies (a folder name per object, owned by the Storage service). Not run against production: the policies change there when the migration merges and `ALTER POLICY` locks each table until the migration commits (half a second is migration 525's figure for 796 policies on an empty database; 526's 221 policies were not timed separately; the lock timeout is 15 seconds); a signed-in scan of a large client-keyed table should be orders of magnitude faster and nothing should answer differently. No screen was timed against the new expression: the figures are counts over a synthetic table.

- **EVERY BROWSER READ THAT NOTHING BOUNDS IS PAGED OR NAMED WITH THE REASON IT CANNOT OUTGROW THE CEILING, AND THE LIST OF NAMES IS A TEST** (POST-A-135, 31-10-2026). PostgREST cuts a response at about 1,000 rows and says nothing, so a browser read with no limit, range, single or pager goes quietly short once its table outgrows the cap, and a list, a pick-list or a total drawn from it is wrong with no sign. The earlier slices paged the reads somebody had found; the ledger carried "about 60 reads, judged bounded and not read individually" until a scanner (`scripts/unboundedReads.ts`) found all 57 and every one was read. 26 were paged: the reads whose rows accrue with a firm's volume (the client list and the pick-lists that read it directly, health scores and alerts, MCA filings, companies and directors, the firm's income-tax notices, loans and fixed deposits, which embed the client and so walk by keyset), and the lists that are listed OLDEST FIRST, where the cut drops the newest, the ones that matter (the two allocation modals' documents for a party, a client's compliance calendar). The 31 left are named in `scripts/an-unpaged-browser-read-is-named-with-its-bound.test.ts` with a reason each (one client's own few rows, one engagement's rows, ids already in hand, document numbers in chunks of 200, a row-security-limited own row), and the test fails a new unbounded read, a name whose read was paged or removed, and a count that changed. A read is PAGED only inside an unclosed `selectAll(` or `selectAllKeyset(` call, so a bare read after a closed pager is not excused by it, and comments are stripped with the quote-aware stripper. That last point was a finding: the older guards remove comments with a regex that treats "/*" inside a LINE comment as the start of a block, so a comment saying "not the /api/health/* routes" hid 200 lines of a screen, and elsewhere 50,000 characters of the API client, from every rule that reads them. The paging guard moved to the shared stripper; the other 173 are a separate change. Not done: nothing here makes a read that IS bounded today stay bounded; a table that grows past its reason is found by whoever reads the list.
